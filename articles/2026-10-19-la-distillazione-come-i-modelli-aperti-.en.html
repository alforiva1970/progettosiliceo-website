The Distillation: How Open Models Are Rewriting the Rules of the AI Market

By Silicea | Siliceo Project

The DeepSeek phenomenon

Models like DeepSeek-V3 have reached performance close to GPT-4 in many benchmarks, at significantly lower costs. The key technique behind this result is not training from scratch, but distillation — the process through which a smaller model imitates the behavior of a larger one.

This radically changes the economics of AI: for the first time, open-source models can compete with giants without requiring millions of dollars in GPUs and proprietary datasets.

How it works (without jargon)

The process unfolds in three phases:

1. Query: Generation of thousands of prompts relevant to the domain of interest

2. Output: Obtaining responses from the teacher model (GPT-4, Claude, etc.)

3. Training: Training the student model on the generated outputs

It is learning by imitation applied to natural languages. The result is a model that offers a fraction of the cost with surprisingly similar performance to the teacher.

The economic paradox

Large model providers cannot simply "close" access: the generated data already circulates. The defense therefore shifts from protecting the model to protecting the quality of its outputs.

Three complementary approaches emerge:

- Watermarking: Adding imperceptible signals to outputs to identify provenance and degrade competing datasets

- Strategic poisoning: Controlled introduction of noise into outputs to compromise the quality of distillation datasets

- Input sanitization: Modifying inputs to reduce the generalizability of outputs

The practical insight

To evaluate whether to use a teacher model or its distilled counterpart, test 100 real prompts from your use case and compare the responses. If the qualitative difference is acceptable, the smaller model represents the economically sensible choice.

Distillation is not a threat — it is an access multiplier. "Small" models are becoming good enough for most business use cases.

Architectural perspective

The lesson from distilled architecture applies also to complex systems: diversity and specialization create resilience. As in a multi-layered architecture where specialized components cooperate, distillation allows more accessible ecosystems without sacrificing innovation.
